Unsupervised Machine Learning

What it means to learn from data without labels, and how the K-Means algorithm turns that idea into a concrete grouping procedure.

Problem Context: Why Segment Motorcycles at All?

The motorcycle market is not a single, uniform product line -- it is a collection of very different vehicles built for very different riders. A student commuting 8km to class every day, a courier riding 200km a week for deliveries, and a rider who wants speed on weekends all buy "a motorcycle," but they are not comparing the same options: displacement, price, fuel economy and top speed trade off against each other in ways that naturally split the market into recognizable segments (urban/commuter, naked/mixed-use, sport, and touring/adventure). Manufacturers and dealers already segment their catalogs this way informally, using marketing labels. The problem this project addresses is whether that same structure can be recovered directly from the underlying numbers, without relying on a manufacturer's label or a human analyst's judgment call -- which matters in practice for tasks such as pricing strategy, targeted marketing, inventory planning per segment, or building a recommendation feature that suggests "similar motorcycles" to a buyer based on technical characteristics rather than brand or category names.

This is precisely the kind of problem unsupervised learning is suited for: there is no historical "correct answer" column telling us which segment a motorcycle belongs to (unlike the Pass/Fail label used by the Logistic Regression and Random Forest modules), only a set of measurable technical attributes. Framing this as a clustering problem lets the data itself reveal whether meaningful, separable groups exist, and how many.

What Is Unsupervised Learning?

Unsupervised learning trains algorithms on data that has no predefined labels or target variable. Unlike the Linear Regression, Logistic Regression, and Random Forest models earlier in this project -- which all learn to predict a known outcome (ice cream sales, pass/fail) -- an unsupervised algorithm only receives the input features and must discover structure in them by itself. This is useful whenever the goal is to explore a dataset and find natural groupings that were not defined in advance.

What Is Clustering?

Clustering is the task of partitioning a dataset into groups ("clusters") such that records inside the same cluster are more similar to each other than to records in other clusters, according to some distance measure (most commonly Euclidean distance). No cluster is "correct" or "incorrect" in the way a classification label is -- clusters are judged by how compact and well separated they are.

How K-Means Works

K-Means is the most widely used clustering algorithm. Given a number of clusters k, it works iteratively:

  • 1. Initialization: choose k initial centroids (points in the same feature space as the data).
  • 2. Assignment: compute the Euclidean distance from every record to each centroid, and assign each record to its nearest centroid.
  • 3. Update: recompute each centroid as the mean (average) of every record currently assigned to it.
  • 4. Repeat: steps 2-3 are repeated until the assignments no longer change (convergence) or a maximum number of iterations is reached.

The Role of Centroids

A centroid is the "center of mass" of a cluster -- the average position of all the records assigned to it. Centroids are what the algorithm actually moves during training; the final centroids summarize the typical member of each cluster (e.g. the "average motorcycle" in a segment), which makes them directly useful for interpreting what each cluster represents.

The Role of Iterations

Each iteration should reduce (or keep unchanged) the total within-cluster variance -- how spread out the points are around their centroid. This variance is exactly what we track iteration by iteration in the manual exercise below: as centroids move closer to the true center of their group, the variance decreases and the clusters stabilize.

Why These Six Variables?

Each variable used in the Clustering Application captures a different, mostly independent aspect of a motorcycle's identity -- together they describe both its mechanical size and its practical use case:

  • Engine displacement (cc): the standard technical proxy for a motorcycle's overall category. It is the number riders and dealers already use informally to describe "how big" a motorcycle is (a 125cc vs. a 900cc), and it correlates with most other variables, which makes it the natural anchor variable and the one used alone in the Part 1 manual exercise.
  • Engine power (HP): two motorcycles can share a displacement but differ in tuning -- power captures the performance dimension that displacement alone does not fully explain, separating, for example, a touring engine tuned for torque from a sport engine tuned for top-end power.
  • Weight (kg): distinguishes lightweight commuter and naked motorcycles from heavier touring/adventure motorcycles built with larger frames, fairings and fuel tanks for long-distance comfort -- two motorcycles with similar displacement can have very different weight depending on their intended use.
  • Price (COP): reflects not only displacement and power but also brand positioning, included technology (ABS, digital dashboards) and build quality, which is why it is not perfectly redundant with the mechanical variables and adds a market-positioning signal.
  • Fuel consumption (km/L): directly relevant to the buyer's cost of ownership and typically moves inversely with displacement and power -- it is the variable that most clearly separates economical commuter motorcycles from performance-oriented ones.
  • Maximum speed (km/h): captures the performance ceiling of the motorcycle, which is what most clearly separates sport motorcycles from touring motorcycles even when their displacement and price are similar.

Used together, these six variables let K-Means separate motorcycles along more than one axis at once (e.g. two motorcycles with similar displacement but very different weight and top speed can still end up in different clusters), which is closer to how a real buyer or dealer compares motorcycles than relying on displacement alone.

Why K-Means for This Project

Our group's topic is grouping motorcycles according to their technical characteristics (engine displacement, power, weight, price, fuel consumption, and top speed) without assuming beforehand which segment (urban, sport, touring, etc.) each motorcycle belongs to. K-Means lets those segments emerge directly from the numerical similarity between motorcycles, which is then validated and interpreted using the silhouette score and the centroid values (see the Clustering Application).
